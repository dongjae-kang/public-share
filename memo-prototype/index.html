1 · The claim the market holds

On 11 October, eight producers holding more than 90 percent of China's effective polysilicon capacity met in Hangzhou and agreed that nothing would sell below 43,000 yuan a tonne. Five days later the front-month contract on the Guangzhou Futures Exchange settled at 34,285. The quote and the price sit 20 percent apart, and the association that publishes the quote counts two or three firms transacting at it. One of those two numbers is wrong.

Author's paragraph. What the equity market has priced on the floor: the move in the solar ETFs on the headlines, the sell-side line that effective capacity contracts by 28 to 40 percent, and the re-rating this implies for Tongwei, GCL and Daqo.

2 · Mechanism

A floor announced by private firms binds only if someone enforces it. In January the State Administration for Market Regulation summoned the same producers and barred any agreement on capacity, output or price, and the consolidation vehicle they had registered in December was halted. The October floor rests instead on the Price Law's clause against selling below cost and on a cost model written by the trade association in July.

Converted without VAT, 43,000 yuan a tonne is about 5.32 dollars a kilogram. Daqo's second quarter put cash cost at 4.57 and total cost at 5.95. The floor clears cash cost and misses full cost, even for one of the lowest-cost producers. It is a cash-cost floor presented as a cost floor, and it is being held by not selling.

Author's paragraph. Why the floor broke in June when Daqo resumed sales below cost, and why five months of stock makes the same dilemma worse now.

3 · Winners and losers

The floors that bind are taxes. Beijing removed the export VAT rebate on modules on 1 April. Washington's Section 232 minimum import price of 0.38 dollars a watt takes effect on 4 December, against a Chinese FOB price near 0.107. The first shrinks China's export volume (module exports are down 9.6 percent year on year through September). The second sets the US price by law for anyone outside China, whatever happens in Hangzhou.

Author's paragraphs. First order: who is insulated (a US manufacturer with a 45 GW backlog and no polysilicon), who is exposed (a levered module exporter with Southeast Asian plants now under AD/CVD). Second order: wafer and cell makers who get cheap feedstock and cannot use it; buyers outside the US who still pay 0.107.
